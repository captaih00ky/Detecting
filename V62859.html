"Live map" shows real streets/terrain under your position, and the LIDAR overlay shades in subtle relief — hollows, banks, old field boundaries — from the Environment Agency's 1m-resolution national survey (covers ~99% of England). Zooming in further than the data's native detail just magnifies existing pixels rather than revealing anything new — 1m is the ceiling for this public dataset, but that's already sharp enough to pick out plough ridges, old boundary banks and hollow-ways. Both live map and LIDAR need a data or wifi signal to load; the offline sketch is always available with no signal at all. Full screen expands the map to fill your whole screen for easier reading in the field — tap "Exit full screen" or your device's back gesture to return.

Tip: log a point every time you get a solid signal, whether you dig it or not — over a session this builds a real map of where the good ground is, not just where your finds were.

Stock programs, explained

Exact names/order shift a little between firmware versions — check your remote's program list against these, but the intent behind each stays the same.

General

The factory default and the right starting point on any ground you don't know yet. It runs a balanced multi-frequency mix so it isn't specialised for depth, small gold, or iron-heavy land — it's the one to load first on a new permission, get a feel for the ground and the junk level, and only move off once you know what you're actually dealing with.

Sensitive

Pushes toward the higher end of the frequency range, which favours small and low-conductivity targets — thin gold rings, small hammered coins, tiny relics. Costs you a little stability in mineralised or electrically noisy ground in exchange for that small-target sensitivity, so it shines on quiet permission land more than on a busy iron-strewn site.

Sensitive FT

Same frequency bias as Sensitive, but running Full Tones audio — every conductivity band gets its own distinct pitch rather than being grouped into 3–5 tones. Gives you much more audio detail to learn from, at the cost of a noisier, busier listening experience; better suited to someone who already knows the ground and wants maximum information from every signal.

Deep / Deep HC

Biased toward lower frequencies and lower reactivity, which is what buys depth on larger, mid-to-high conductivity targets (bigger coins, cupro-nickel, larger relics). HC (high conductivity) leans even further that way. Trade-off is reduced sensitivity to small, low-conductivity targets — a good choice once you suspect a field has been hunted hard at normal depth and you want to see what's sitting deeper.

Relic

Built around frequency subtraction — comparing responses across frequencies to strip out the ground and iron signal, leaving larger, older, often lower-conductivity targets more visible underneath. Reactivity is usually kept low here, which favours depth over fast target separation, so it's suited to sparser, older sites rather than trashy modern land where lots of targets sit close together.

Beach / Beach Sens

Tuned to reject the fast, salt-driven false signals that wet sand produces, which a land program will struggle with. Beach Sens adds extra sensitivity to small non-ferrous targets (jewellery, small coins) on top of that salt rejection — the pair to use any time you're within reach of the tideline, wet sand, or salt-damp ground.

Gold Field

Prospecting-oriented: sensitivity is weighted toward the low-conductivity end of the scale where small gold nuggets sit, since nuggets often read close to iron or ground signal and get thrown away by normal discrimination. Expect more chatter in mineralised ground in exchange for not missing genuinely small, low-conductivity gold.

Diving

Configured for stability underwater and in saltwater specifically — resistant to the false signals salt water causes, and biased toward rings and coins over foil, which is the dominant junk category underwater. Not a land program; only relevant if you're detecting submerged.

Deus Mono

Runs a single frequency (rather than the usual multi-frequency blend) with an extended range and stronger EMI rejection. Worth switching to when multi-frequency operation itself becomes the problem — heavy electrical interference, or ground so mineralised that multi-frequency processing gets unstable — since a single clean frequency can outperform multi-frequency in exactly those conditions.

Legacy 4.2

A free, community-built custom program (not a stock XP program) built around frequency management to pull old, low-conductivity targets — Saxon, medieval and Roman coins in particular — cleanly out of iron-heavy ground. Detectorists mainly reach for it on old pasture, deserted medieval village sites, and other historically "dirty" land where a stock program either buries the target in iron noise or discriminates it away entirely.

Because it's a downloadable program rather than a menu option, install it through the official NoxEditor program installer (or by entering the program code on your remote, if you were given one) rather than trying to rebuild it from memory — exact tone-break and reactivity values get refined between releases, so the authoritative version is whatever the current install file gives you.

Forager 4

Another free community program, built around "morphing" — it blends frequency behaviour as you sweep rather than sitting on one fixed profile. That makes it a forgiving general-purpose choice across mixed or changing ground (a field that goes from dry topsoil to a damp hollow, for instance), which is why people tend to reach for it as a do-it-all option on a new permission before settling into something more specialised.

Same advice as Legacy: install via the official program file/code rather than hand-entering settings, so you get whatever the current tuned release actually is.

Both are free downloads distributed through XP's dealer network and community channels (search "XP Deus 2 Legacy 4.2" / "Forager 4" for the current install code and video walkthrough) — worth re-checking before a big trip in case a newer revision has replaced the one you have.

How this app stores your data

No cookies, and nothing is sent anywhere. Your track, finds, and VDI log are saved with a browser feature called localStorage — a small local database the page keeps entirely on your phone, tied to this file/address. There's no server behind this app at all; everything happens in the browser tab itself.

Practical points worth knowing:

  • It persists between visits — closing the tab or restarting your phone won't lose it.
  • It's tied to this exact file or web address. If you move the file, re-host it at a new URL, or open it in a different browser, that counts as a different "origin" and starts with an empty store.
  • "Clear browsing data" in your browser settings will wipe it, and on some phones (iOS Safari in particular) storage for infrequently-opened sites can be cleared automatically after a long period of inactivity.
  • Because of that, use the backup tool below — it saves everything to an actual JSON file on your phone, independent of browser storage entirely.

Backup & restore (local JSON file)

This saves your entire track, finds log, and VDI log into one .json file on your phone — not tied to a browser, an origin, or this specific copy of the app. It's the safest way to carry your data between devices, hosting changes, or a fresh install.

Exporting downloads a file to your Files/Downloads folder — move it into iCloud Drive or another folder you trust for safekeeping. Restoring reads a previously exported file and replaces the app's current track/finds/VDI data with what's in it, so only restore a backup you mean to load in full.

Reporting what you find (England, Wales & NI)

Under the Treasure Act 1996, an object counts as Treasure if it's at least 300 years old and either: it's not a coin and contains at least 10% gold or silver by weight; or it's part of a group of two or more coins of that age containing that much precious metal (or ten or more coins of any precious-metal content); or it's a group of two or more prehistoric base-metal objects found together. Anything found alongside a Treasure item counts as Treasure too, regardless of what it's made of.

If you think you've found Treasure, you're legally required to report it to your local coroner — in practice, this means contacting your local Finds Liaison Officer (part of the Portable Antiquities Scheme), who handles the process for you. You have 14 days from realising it might qualify to report it.

Scotland works differently — there's no 10%/300-year test; anything of possible archaeological or historical significance should be reported to the Treasure Trove Unit.

Even when something isn't legally Treasure, recording it with your local Finds Liaison Officer (England & Wales) is voluntary but is how individual finds end up contributing to the wider archaeological record — worth doing for anything old or unusual. And always agree permission and a finds-sharing arrangement with the landowner before you start.

PAS-style export

Formats your finds log into the fields the Portable Antiquities Scheme's online recording form asks for, including an approximate British National Grid reference calculated from each find's GPS location — ready to copy in when you report a find. Material and broad period are left for you to fill in, since those need expert eyes.

The grid reference is calculated on your phone using the standard published conversion formula, accurate to within a few metres — good enough to locate a findspot, but not survey-grade.
